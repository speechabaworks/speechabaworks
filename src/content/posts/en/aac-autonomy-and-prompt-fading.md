Here is the conversation, more or less verbatim, in a hundred different buildings.

The speech-language pathologist says the child must have unrestricted access to their device. Not access when it is time to work. Not access when a communication target has been programd. Access, the way a speaking child has access to their mouth.

The behavior analyst says that access without a fading plan produces a child who can only communicate when an adult is standing beside them, and that we owe this child independence rather than a lifelong prompt.

Both of these people are right, and both of them think the other one has missed the point.

## What each side is actually protecting

The SLP is protecting the child's **right to say something nobody asked them to say**. This is not sentimentality. A device that only comes out during structured sessions teaches, very efficiently, that communication is a thing you do for adults on request. Children learn that lesson quickly and it is expensive to undo.

The BCBA is protecting the child's **independence from the adult**. Prompt dependence is real, it is measurable, and a child who has been hand-over-handed through two years of requesting has not learned to request — they have learned to wait for a hand.

Notice that neither of those is a position about AAC. They are positions about who the communication belongs to. And they only conflict if you assume the plan has to treat every moment of the day the same way.

## The distinction that dissolves it

Split the day. Not by activity, and not by who is running the session — by **who initiated**.

When the child initiates, there is no fading schedule, because there is no prompt. The child reached for the device on their own. Anything you do at that moment other than respond is a lesson that initiating does not work. Model, expand, respond — but do not run a trial.

When the adult initiates a teaching opportunity, run the prompt hierarchy properly. Least-to-most, transfer trials, the whole apparatus, with data. That is where fading belongs, and it belongs there rigorously.

Written into a plan, that is two sentences:

> Device is available at all times and all child-initiated communication receives an immediate natural response, without prompting or trial structure.
>
> Adult-initiated instructional trials follow the prompt hierarchy in section 4, with fading criteria at 80% independence across three sessions.

Neither professional has conceded anything. The SLP has unrestricted access. The BCBA has a fading schedule with criteria. What they have given up is the assumption that one rule has to govern the entire day.

## Why this keeps happening anyway

Because the two plans are written separately, by two people, in two documents, and neither document says what happens in the other one.

Almost every collaboration failure I am called into looks like a clinical disagreement and turns out to be a documentation problem. Two competent professionals, each with a coherent internal model, who have never had to reconcile their models on one page — so the reconciliation happens implicitly, in the classroom, by whichever adult is in the room at the time.

That is not collaboration. That is alternating.

If you take one thing from this: the test of an integrated plan is not whether both disciplines contributed to it. It is whether a teaching assistant who has read only that document knows what to do when the child picks up the device unprompted at 10:40 on a Tuesday.
